Context

Smart glasses are a crowded space, and the crowd has a type.

Clover is a personal AI agent that lives in a pair of camera-free smart glasses and a companion iPhone app. When I joined there was no fixed branding, no interaction model and no design system. There were two co-founders with two different pictures of how a person should talk to the glasses, and a market whose perception of the category had been set by Meta: invasive, camera first, for a particular kind of person.

Problem

Too many directions to lead with, and no data yet to choose between them.

Three problems were tangled together, and I had to pull them apart before I could design anything.

  • The interaction model. Intent models, app-first, voice-first. One co-founder believed in an intention model with voice, giving the user full control of the glasses by speaking. The other believed in a ring-based app interaction, where the user scrolls through the glasses the way Rokid does. I owned the experience, so the decision landed on me.
  • The audience. Who are these glasses for, and can one design system serve 95 percent of their use cases without becoming two products?
  • Perception and brand. Because of Meta, smart glasses read as invasive. Clover has no camera, which is the answer to the privacy objection, but the brand had to say so, and we did not have a brand. A personal agent also raises its own questions: how should its chats appear, and how are its capabilities presented?

Research

The shape of the HUD and the brand decided who bought the glasses.

I started with a competitor analysis of the brands that had worked: Rokid, RayNeo, Even Realities and [others]. The interesting finding was that the shape of the HUD and the branding had shaped each company's audience. The majority of buyers were utilitarian, technical, usually founders or people far enough into their careers to want a secretary of their own.

We wanted to break that. The glasses should be for everyday use by everyone, and we had use cases for people who are visually impaired or colour-blind for whom the technology matters more than it does to anyone in tech. Hence the research question became whether one design could hold both groups.

INTERVIEW CARD 02 · NON-TECHNICAL · 6 AUGUST 2026

Anthony Fox, data scientist at a European startup

Economics graduate, market sizing and expansion advisory. Interested in hardware, no engineering background. Previously owned a Withings ScanWatch 2 and stopped wearing it. Paraphrased from the session notes.

What came up

  • Wants wearables that are analog in feel, good looking and unobtrusive. Deliberately avoids devices that label him a technologist or draw attention in professional and academic settings.
  • Hardware should reinforce structure in his day and reduce reliance on the phone, saving five to ten minutes across routine tasks, rather than substitute for the phone.
  • Not looking for new tools for the working day. Wants structure and comfort in personal time, the evening, the laundry.
  • Limits notifications for Slack and mail on purpose, then opens them anyway for fear of missing a client. He named the paradox himself.
  • Objective data such as sleep scores made him feel guilty when it observed without offering a fix.
  • Stopped using the ScanWatch over a lost proprietary charger and no felt change to his routine.

On the prototypes

  • Preferred designs that gave immediate instructions or a structured timeline. Disliked anything overwhelming, overly technological, or showing unnecessary information such as the time.
  • Strong preference for a timeline he could check tasks off, for the sense of accomplishment without the negative reinforcement of missed goals. Less interested in black-themed or feature-heavy layouts.
  • His chosen configuration: the memory layer or timeline first, then an ask function, then profile and settings, with a clear battery indicator.
  • Glasses for immediate, high-priority tasks. Phone for the high-level calendar, settings, music, photos and messaging.
  • Asked for a morning wake-up greeting and smart home integration as reasons to adopt.

What it changed. The memory layer leads. The split between what the glasses show and what the phone manages came straight out of this session. The time disappeared from the default display.

What the testing told me

Non-technical users leaned towards a fun, interactive, creative presentation of the agent in their glasses. The technical users wanted the utilitarian version with plain graphics. I was operating at an awkward intersection: half of our first intended audience was allergic to fun, and the audience we needed in the long run was drawn to it. The question became how to hit the sweet spot, which is a design system that scales from one to the other without becoming two products.

Two things in the notes cut across both groups, and they mattered more than the split. Everyone limited notifications on purpose and still checked compulsively. Everyone wanted the glasses for the immediate moment and the phone for anything that needed management. [If the other five sessions agree, say so with the count.]

The reading, and the first decision

To settle app against intent I read the research rather than picking a side in the founders' argument: Mark, Gonzalez and Harris on task switching (CHI 2005), Laput and Harrison on sensing what hands are doing to avoid interruption (CHI 2019), Sahami Shirazi on which notifications people value (CHI 2014), Pizza and Visuri on glance length on wearables (CHI 2016, 2017), Bailey and Konstan on interruption cost, Horvitz on mixed-initiative thresholds, Weiser and Seely Brown on calm technology, NASA's work on cognitive tunnelling, the DCMP caption standards, Microsoft's HAX guidelines, Google's PAIR guide and Apple's generative-AI guidance. [Add the Microsoft papers you mean specifically; "over fifteen" should be a list you can show.]

The papers said an app model gives clarity and structure. Every participant was drawn to the idea of an app because it was something they already knew. Hence it made sense that people would take to a system they recognised; you do not redesign an interface for the sake of it, you adapt it to what the user already knows. I pushed back on voice-first and went app-first, and started prototyping.

Development

Built the app-first HUD, loaded it on the glasses, and started the brand from nothing.

  • The HUD. An app-first display, prototyped in the browser and loaded onto the RayNeo. [Name the inspirations for the first display here.]
  • The agent's face. Customisable orbs as the agent's presence, because customisation was what the non-technical testers asked for; the orb morphs into each app's icon when it acts.
  • The brand. Nature, because our philosophy is to give a person more intent and care through their day, paired with the Japanese idea of ikigai and the art of slowly noticing. It is almost a contradiction for a productivity device, and that is deliberate: a reminder to users that we hear them, in a market where everyone else optimises the day rather than slows it down.
  • The app. Modes of focus, an idea that came from a testing participant who wanted to adjust what the glasses showed from his phone. To make it truly customisable, users drag and drop which apps appear on their glasses, in the order they want.

Testing

The storm was not over. Shipping to real wearers started it.

Once we handed out pairs of glasses, the live feedback loop asked for more iteration than the usability sessions had. Two things went wrong at once.

The interaction model. App-first forces the wearer to arrive with intent already formed. The live data said that was friction: 86 percent of users [of how many, over what period, measured how] found the app-based interaction restrictive for exactly that reason. That is not how people use these glasses. You pick them up first thing in the morning and you need help shaping your intent. Nobody says "open calendar app"; they ask "what do I have today". Human language is fluid, not structural, and my job is to shape the interface to that. So I pushed back on my own decision and went to explore intent models.

The codebase. I had built the first HUD in vanilla CSS on the assumption that a coloured display made that workable. Loading it on the glasses proved otherwise, since the chips need their own SDK, and merging my prototype into the engineers' codebase cost us time. For the second direction I rebuilt in [TypeScript and the exact stack; confirm], which merged cleanly.

The A/B rounds. [Describe what was compared, app-first against intent-first prompts, how many wearers, over how long, and what you measured. The direction is clear in your notes; the numbers have to be yours.] After the rounds, the finding was that a mix of the two should exist, and that the mix is the sweet spot for the addressable market.

Solution

A minimal HUD, because the glass has to be legible to everyone. The fun lives in the app.

The HUD became more minimal, not from taste but from the testing and from accessibility: a display on a pair of glasses has to be readable at a glance by people with low vision and colour blindness as well as by founders. Intent comes first on the glass, so the wearer can ask "what do I have today" and get an answer, and the app structure sits underneath for the moments when someone knows exactly what they want. The creative, customisable side, the orbs, the modes, the drag and drop, lives on the phone, where there is room for it.

Key takeaways

  1. It is easy to be carried away by noise in usability testing and by trends. Choose a north star metric so the functional decisions are driven by data. [Name yours.]
  2. Re-pivots and iterations are the most fundamental part of the process. They are the hypothesis tested in the open. The best ideas are not always the first ones, and app-first was mine.
  3. Sync with engineering and the founders earlier than feels necessary. I would have saved the engineering team real time by building in the right language from the start instead of making my code hard to merge. The most important conversations hide in the back of everyone's head. Align, engage and keep asking until everything is set out, rather than revisited.

Next steps

  1. Validate the mixed model with the wearers who rejected app-first. A one-week diary study with the same technical and non-technical split, measuring how often an intent prompt gets the wearer what they wanted on the first try, against the app route.
  2. Instrument the HUD for the north star. Count glances and accepted interruptions per day rather than time on device, since every tester told us they want less screen time, not more.
  3. Ship customisation to the test cohort and measure phone dependence. Modes and the memory layer were the two things all three documented testers asked for. Shipping them and measuring whether phone pickups fall is the test of the product's own claim.
