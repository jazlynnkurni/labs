DRAFT · CLOVER CASE STUDY · JAZLYNN KURNIANDRA · OCTOBER 2026

Working draft assembled from the Clover design sessions, August to October 2026. Every number in here is one that survived the audit. Items marked [confirm] are inferred and need checking before this goes anywhere.

The phone has to do the thinking the glasses are not allowed to do.

Clover is a proactive assistant for smart glasses, an assistant that speaks first rather than waiting to be asked. The glasses get two seconds of your attention. Everything else is a mobile design problem, and that is the part I owned.

Role
Founding product designer and design engineer. Solo designer against one engineer's live backend. [confirm]		 Duration

The Modes feature, in SwiftUI, in the production iPhone app. One HUD build running on a RayNeo X3 Pro.		 Built

Context

Every assistant is built for the moment you ask, and that moment is already solved.

Your phone answers questions well. A screen on your face that also answers questions is a worse phone strapped to your head. Hence the product cannot be the asking. It has to be the moment you never asked for, where the assistant speaks first and is right.

Clover runs on RayNeo X3 Pro glasses: a see-through display, 640 by 480 pixels per eye, about 24 by 18 degrees of your view. It is paired to an iPhone app that I designed and partly built. I joined as the only designer, working against a backend one engineer was building at the same time.

The user who set every rule

Priya is a physiotherapist in Melbourne. Her hands are on a patient six hours a day. Her Apple Watch has been in a drawer since January, because a standing reminder buzzed mid-session and the patient saw her glance at it. Her words: "It made me feel like a bad clinician."

She is not buying a screen. She is buying not having to switch gears. Her quit condition is one sentence: it spoke while my hands were on someone. This means she will never open an app mid-session, so the phone is a later surface, for the gap between patients. It also means that interruption is the thing most likely to kill the product, rather than a feature of it.

Honest note: one persona built from interviews, not a cohort. Stated again under outcomes.

Problem

A proactive assistant is wrong a lot. The phone is where that gets repaired.

Glasses are good at one glance. They are poor at everything that follows, which is checking what it said, correcting it and teaching it. If the phone does not hold the record, a wrong answer is just a bad memory.

  • User problem. It spoke at the wrong moment and she has no way to tell it so.
  • Business problem. Hardware sells once. An assistant that cannot be corrected does not improve, so it never earns daily use, and daily use is what makes glasses a product rather than a gadget.
  • Design problem. Correction is admin. Nobody opens an app to do admin.

The constraint that is physics, not taste

A see-through display adds light to the scene and cannot emit darkness, so black costs nothing and a bright plate is glare. I sized the whole system in degrees of vision rather than pixels, because on a near-eye display a pixel has no fixed size. The panel gives 26.7 pixels per degree, and type is measured in arcminutes of cap height, an arcminute being one sixtieth of a degree, with nothing below 16 arcminutes. Three numbers then decided the design for me.

Two seconds
The glance budget. About three short words. You control glance count, not duration. More text makes people look twice, which is worse.		 50 against a floor of 70
Our brand green scores 50 on Google's tone scale for legibility on glass. The brand colour physically fails on the display. One colour, and it is not ours.		 47 degrees to 21
Monochrome against full colour on one plate. Colour costs more than half the view, so colour can never signal urgency here.

Solution

The glass owns the moment. The phone owns the record and the repair.

One sentence, and it settles almost every scoping argument before it starts. I wrote it after auditing what the backend could actually send against what a two-second glance can actually hold.

Stays on the glass
  • Capture, one fast answer and one useful interruption
  • Yes or no, or two candidates. Never an open question
 Must be the phone, because the glass physically cannot
  • Sign-in, permissions and anything needing a browser or keyboard
  • Five-option choices and free text. The backend produces them, the glass has no frame that can take them
  • Lists. A tool returns fifty items, the glass can show eight
  • Anything you would want to re-read

That audit found nine collisions between what the server can send and what the glass can show. Each is now an engineering ticket, caught in design rather than QA.

Five screens, each with a written job

  • Today. Present tense. Exactly one turn, no scrollback. Voice lives here.
  • The Record. Everything Clover said, not just what you asked. The only place anything gets corrected.
  • What Clover Knows. The rules it has learned from your corrections, in one list you can edit.
  • Modes. Which apps reach the glasses, per context. Built and shipped.
  • Devices. Pairing, battery, and what is allowed to interrupt.

Shipped: Modes. Drag an app in, and it leaves the shelf.

You build a mode by dragging apps into a tray. The tray's order is the order they appear on the glasses, so each slot is its own drop target rather than one bucket. Past five apps it warns you. That is not a guess: seven fit the glasses carousel and only three are ever legible at once, so a sixth app makes a mode you cannot scan. The research is in the component. It shipped into the production app behind two entry points, because burying it one level deeper made it unfindable in testing.

The Dynamic Island: I measured the space before designing for it. Half the pill is hardware.

Most people design the Island from the marketing render. I built a ruler at true point size and typed real content into it. The island is 250 points wide. The camera takes 125.34 of it. What is left is two regions of 62.33 points that cannot be joined. At Apple's 11 point floor that is about eleven characters a side. "Clover 24m" is already the entire budget, and that one measurement decided every layout that followed.

I drew eight formats at true size, and five survived. Works: a task in progress, ready-needs-you, a count, a timer, the glasses mode. Tight: navigation, where "240ft" fits but "Turn left on Gough" is twenty-one characters against a budget of eleven. Fails: live captions, the feature reviewers love most, because a sentence needs a line, not eleven characters twice with a camera in between. The pattern is that the Island rewards state and count and punishes language.

The opening nobody has taken. ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity, Copilot, Grok and Siri all use the Dynamic Island as a microphone-on light. Apple's own proactive assistant puts its suggestions on the Watch. Meanwhile a server has been able to open a Live Activity, the iOS mechanism that lets an app post a live status to the Island and the lock screen, with the app closed since iOS 17.2. The door has been open for two years. Hence the position is that Clover opens the Island when something is about to matter and closes it when it does not, rather than that our widgets live there. iOS keeps that honest. A Live Activity dies at eight hours in code, the sandbox has no network so it can display but not think, and since June Apple names Live Activities in the unsolicited-messages rule. So the user arms it; a nudge she asked for is defensible, and one she did not is exactly what that rule now covers.

Interaction craft: the card keystones rather than shrinks.

For the card-to-Island transition I pulled a reference animation apart frame by frame. The card is a trapezoid in transit, top edge wide, bottom narrowing. That is a rotation anchored at the top edge plus a rise and a scale. A uniform scale reads as shrinking, which is the cheap version. The shadow is a separate object travelling half the card's distance; that parallax tells you the card has left the surface. It fades before the card does, because something that far off the paper stops casting anything. The CSS transform-origin at the top edge maps one to one onto SwiftUI's anchor top, which is why the browser prototype carried straight into the app.

How I got there

What went wrong first: I built something beautiful before earning the right to show anything.

The first thing I made for the glasses was a 360 degree garden where every grain of the point cloud is a four-leaf clover, three hundred thousand of them making the trees, the ground and the bridge. It is the best-looking thing in the project. On the glass it is wrong, since a lit field is glare, and glare is the opposite of not switching gears. It moved to the phone. The lesson shaped everything after it, which is to build the constraint first and the beauty second.

Iteration: a grammar instead of screens

Instead of designing screens for the glasses I designed six frames, a grammar whereby every scenario is assembled from the same small set of displays, and wrote twelve scenarios as sequences of them. 0 Dark, nothing lit, the default and most of every scenario. 1 Statement, one line, no response wanted, expires by itself. 2 Ask, one line, yes or no. 3 Pick, two candidates, never an open question. 4 Plate and panel, only when there genuinely is an image. 5 Live, the only frame with motion, for capture and dictation. If a feature cannot be spelled in this alphabet it does not belong on the glass. The grammar is what let one designer and one engineer stay aligned without a spec review: a feature arrives already written as 0 5 3 2.

Trade-off: dark at rest, against the entire category

What the rest of the category ships is a permanent status pane, with time, battery, weather and notifications. It makes the hardware feel alive and it demos well. What it costs is that the wearer is being spoken to constantly, so the one moment that matters has no way to stand out. What I shipped is nothing until Clover has something worth the glance. What it costs is that it demos badly; an empty screen is a hard thing to sell in a meeting and I argued for it twice. It wins because scarcity is the only thing that makes an interruption legible. If it is always talking, Priya cannot tell when it matters.

Trade-off: I kept the screen whose success metric is going unused

A log of past conversations is the most commonly ignored screen in any assistant. I proposed deleting The Record twice. It survived both times for one reason, which is that Clover is mostly wrong proactively, and the only surface designed for correcting it could not see the thing most in need of correction. So The Record holds everything it said, not just what she asked. Correction is three taps, and the third is answered for her, since a wrong-time mistake defaults to always and a wrong fact to just this once. Answering does not delete the entry. It stays, with a "Learned" line and an undo, so she can see the fix stuck. Its usage decaying is the success metric, not churn. Defending a feature that should get quieter over time is the judgement call I am proudest of.

The glasses, when they do speak: three to five actions at once

"Hey Clover, put in my to-do for the launch at 3, update my calendar, and text Rita to be ready." The HUD had a working language for one action at a time. For several, the answer was that three moments earn pixels, which are the plan, a progress ring and the receipt. The orb morphs into each app's icon as that action runs and the real widget for that app shows in the plate, nothing bespoke. Measured, the busiest the panel ever gets is before the work starts: 1.8 percent of the display lit, against 6.3 percent for the calendar at rest [unverified: re-measure both panels the same way, coverage or luminance, before quoting].

Research that changed my mind: the ring

I argued for a smart ring as an input device for the glasses. Six research passes later, every ring on the market was an input device and not one sensed context. Laput and Harrison wrote the idea down in one paragraph in 2019, that a system which knows what your hands are doing can avoid interrupting you, and nobody followed up. The sensing is proven, 95 percent across 25 hand activities from a stock watch IMU, and in Fogarty, Hudson and Lai's CHI 2004 study statistical models classified non-interruptible moments at 82.4 percent against 76.9 percent for human judges, a Wizard-of-Oz result from four offices rather than a field trial. I switched the recommendation to a ring that senses whether her hands are on a patient rather than a ring that clicks. I also reported the two findings that cut against it, that every output ring has died since 2018 and that Even Realities solved confirmation in the HUD without a motor.

AI in the process, and the part most people skip

I used AI to go faster, then spent the time I saved checking it. Research agents ran concurrently on competitor hardware, display physics, interruption science and platform limits, twelve passes in all, each returning sourced claims with its gaps named. Fourteen live prototypes instead of static mockups, because a two-second glance budget and a gesture conflict are things you feel, not assert. I wrote the SwiftUI myself and ran it in the simulator; that is how I found that 160 points is a real ceiling for an expanded Island, not a guideline.

My research agents produced fabricated statistics. I keep a list of them. A citation to a paper that does not contain the number attributed to it. A competitor spec scraped from a knockoff marketplace listing. A widely repeated attention statistic with no traceable source. A retention claim that was another vendor's marketing wearing a citation. Every research document I write now ends with a section called Numbers to refuse. It matters more than the findings, since a wrong finding wastes a sprint and a fabricated number in a deck is very hard to walk back. AI fluency is less about how fast you can generate and more about knowing which half to throw away.

Outcomes

  • Fourteen live prototypes. The team reviews behaviour, not pictures.
  • Five screens with a written job each. One of them, Modes, shipped into the production app.
  • Nine backend-to-display collisions found in design rather than QA, now engineering tickets.
  • One rule the team now scopes with. "The glass owns the moment, the phone owns the record." It settles arguments before they start.
  • Research inside components. The five-app warning in Modes carries the reason it exists.
  • One build on real glasses, with the input path measured rather than assumed.

Honest gaps

No longitudinal data. One persona from interviews, not a cohort. The proactive nudge and the interruption threshold are designed but unvalidated. The next study is one physiotherapist, one week, holding out whole people rather than sessions.

What I would carry into any mobile product

  1. Measure the surface before you design for it. Eleven characters a side is a measurement rather than an opinion, and a measurement ends debates that taste cannot.
  2. Write the rule that scopes the next ten features. One sentence about what each surface owns did more for velocity than any flow I drew. The things it could not place were the things to cut.
  3. Speed is only an advantage if you audit it. AI let me run twelve research passes in a week, and it also handed me fabricated numbers. The list of what I threw away is the real artefact.
