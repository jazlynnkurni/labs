Clover smart glasses

Summary

Clover is not an interface that users navigate. It is an agent that negotiates how much interface the user needs at any given moment.

Clover is Eden's first product: a personal AI agent in a pair of camera-free smart glasses, with an iPhone app beside it. I joined as the founding product designer and design engineer, July to October 2026, before there was a settled interaction model, a design system or a brand, on a team of about ten across the US and China. [confirm headcount]

I owned the whole experience: the HUD interaction architecture, the iOS companion app, the design system and the early brand system. I prototyped and implemented, including the app in SwiftUI and voice-interactive prototypes of the glasses. The work moved the product from an app-first HUD, which failed at the first question of the day, to an intent-led model with three levels of interface support, a proposed orchestration, recovery and interruption architecture, a rendering system of six primitives and a fit rule, and a companion app with Modes shipped.

These are design and build outcomes. There is no market data yet, and this document does not claim any. [Add verified usability or live-feedback results here if they exist.]

Context

Smart glasses promise less time on your phone, and most of them deliver one more thing that interrupts you.

We entered a category whose assumptions were already set by Meta: technical, intrusive, camera first, made for enthusiasts. Clover has no camera, which answers the privacy objection, but nobody had decided how a person should talk to the glasses. One co-founder believed in an intention model with voice, giving the wearer full control by speaking. The other believed in a ring-based, app-like model where you scroll through the glasses the way Rokid does. I owned the experience, so the decision landed on me.

The job was to decide what to display, where each interaction should live, and how the whole thing should feel, and to do it without a brand, because branding kept losing to hardware on the priority list.

Opportunity

Making the first thing a person puts on in the morning work for people who do not yet know what they want from it.

I compared Rokid, RayNeo, Even Realities and [others] on interaction mechanism, glanceability, information density and what the visual language signalled about the buyer. The shape of the HUD and the branding had shaped each company's audience: utilitarian, technical, usually founders or people far enough into their careers to want a secretary of their own. One detail mattered later. None of the successful brands imposes a fixed navigation scheme on the HUD.

Eyewear is the most accessible wearable there is, and that cuts both ways. Everyone can put on glasses, so the market is open. But when the glasses are the first thing you use in the day, you do not want to think, and there is no intent yet. A phone is picked up with a purpose. Glasses are put on before the purpose exists. The opportunity was to help people form their intent, not to assume it. We also had use cases for people who are visually impaired or colour-blind, for whom the technology matters more than it does to anyone in tech, which is why one design had to hold both audiences.

I treated the fun-versus-plain split as a hypothesis rather than proof that we needed two products. The signals that held across everyone, control over interruption and a clear division between immediate and deeper tasks, were worth more than taste. That gave me two design questions. How does a HUD help someone form an intent they do not have yet? And how does one design system serve both the people who want nothing and the people who want more?

Deliverables

So, how do we build glasses that decide for themselves when a screen is worth showing?

I proposed an intent-led HUD in which Notes, Calendar, Maps and Messages are services the agent invokes rather than places the wearer goes. The wearer speaks naturally, the agent interprets intent and context, routes to the capability, and answers in whatever form fits: a spoken line, a glanceable card, a full view, or an action. Underneath it sit three levels of interface support, a task orchestration layer kept separate from presentation, a task-state model for interruptions, and a rendering system of six information primitives with one rule for what fits on the panel.

What made this interesting to design is proactivity. A phone waits to be asked, so its designer only has to design for behaviour. Clover speaks first, so I could not stop at what people do; I had to infer what they prefer from what they do, because every unasked-for moment is a bet on a preference the wearer never stated. Designing for behaviour was the floor. Inferring preference from behaviour was the job.

Beside the glasses, the iPhone app owns what the glass physically cannot: onboarding, configuration, Modes with drag-and-drop ordering of the apps that reach the glasses, a search-shaped AI surface for deeper questions, summaries, and error recovery. Across both runs one agent identity and one memory.

Listening and empathising with our users

How the design answers what we heard, in both groups.

For non-technical users

Pain point: a feature list is overwhelming; they want structure and not to look like a technologist.

✓ Nothing shows on the glass until it is worth the glance. The default is Level 1, a spoken line, and the memory layer leads when a visual is needed.

Pain point: notifications are limited on purpose and checked compulsively anyway.

✓ Summaries instead of scrolling, and every long-running task reports what it understood, what it completed and what still needs attention, so there is nothing to go and check.

Pain point: the app felt capable and cold.

✓ The garden direction carries the emotional side of the product on the phone, where there is room for it, while the HUD stays restrained.

For technical users

Pain point: wanted control over what appears on the display without digging through menus on the glasses.

✓ Modes in the app, with drag-and-drop ordering, so priority is expressed directly and the HUD stays simple.

Pain point: wanted the utilitarian version and no decoration on the glass.

✓ The HUD is minimal by rule, not taste. Level 3 has to be earned by context and intent.

What stays the same for both

✓ The glass owns the moment; the phone owns the record and the repair.

✓ The wearer keeps control: every long task is interruptible, and anything that reaches another person asks first.

Interaction principles

Five rules the glasses follow, written before the screens.

Speak naturally, not in app commands. Nobody should have to say Calendar, Notes or Inbox to reach them.

Complexity stays behind the interface. A command with five actions can still receive a one-sentence acknowledgement.

Only surface actionable information. No progress card for every internal operation.

Errors are recoverable locally. Clarify or retry the failed step, never restart the workflow.

The user retains control. Every long-running task is interruptible, and external actions get appropriate confirmation.

HUD // Design iterations

I chose app-first on purpose, loaded it on the glasses, and watched it fail at the first question of the day.

Voice or intent-led interaction can feel fluid, and it also hides what the product can do. An app-first structure is familiar and predictable. I proposed judging the models on learnability, predictability and cognitive load, then prototyped app-first as an explicit hypothesis. The first HUD followed the phone's mental model: the user has a task, navigates to an app or widget, issues a command, the AI answers inside that context.

INSERT IMAGE HERE: assets/hud/flow-dashboard.png and flow-cal.png, the app-first HUD on the glasses

On the glasses that model inherited every assumption of a smartphone. It assumes you know what you want, know which application does it, and deliberately enter that environment. The first question of the day is "what do I have today?", and the app-first HUD had no answer to a question that names no app. Live feedback from wearers said the same thing: being forced to arrive with an intent already formed was friction. [The 86 percent figure stays qualitative until the denominator and the measurement are on record.] So I pushed back on my own decision and moved to the hybrid.

Three levels of interface support

I stopped designing interfaces around application categories and started designing them around the amount of attention a task requires.

  • Level 1, ambient. Quick retrieval, lightweight capture, acknowledgement. One to five seconds, nothing opens. "When is my next meeting?" "Design sync at three."
  • Level 2, glanceable. A compact visual to verify, compare or decide. Five to twenty seconds. "What does my afternoon look like?" gets the next three events, condensed.
  • Level 3, expanded. A persistent surface for sustained workflows, editing, navigation or continuous guidance. "Help me rehearse my presentation" gets a teleprompter with live feedback.

The durations are design targets, and the levels describe the interface support a task needs, not the length of the spoken request. One capability lives at all three. "How far is the nearest pharmacy?" is a spoken distance; "navigate me to the pharmacy" is a persistent map. Treating them as the same interaction is the smartphone assumption again. Level 3 has to be earned by context and intent, never assumed because the task is complicated.

Multi-action orchestration

"Research stem cells, turn the findings into flashcards, and send them to Nita's mom." Three dependent actions in one breath, and the instinct is to show three steps. I separated orchestration from presentation instead. A task layer coordinates the work in the background and the HUD shows only the milestones that mean something to the wearer: the acknowledgement, a sense of progress, the result, and any confirmation that needs her. If she wants to inspect or edit the flashcards, the display earns Level 3. At any point she can say "hide this", "show me more" or "go back" and set the amount of interface herself. Task complexity should not become interface complexity, and on a display this small it physically cannot.

Trust, partial success and consequential actions

"Put in my to-do for the product launch today at three, update my calendar, and text Rita to be ready." The first two actions are reversible, so the agent executes them when the details are clear and the user has authorised it. The third reaches another person, so it asks: "Your launch to-do and calendar event are set for 3 PM. I have drafted Rita's message. Ready to send?" One confirmation where the consequence is external, none where it is not.

Then the harder case: the calendar write fails while the to-do succeeds. The interface has to represent partial success plainly. Hiding a failure on a wearable is how trust ends, and so is "sorry, something went wrong", which leaves the wearer with no idea whether the earlier steps happened. Drawing on Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction, which ask systems to support efficient correction, communicate uncertainty and make their behaviour understandable, I reduced system state to three questions the HUD must answer at any moment: what did you understand, what did you complete, and what still needs my attention. Recovery happens locally, by clarifying or retrying the failed step. [The 88 percent abandonment statistic from the working document has no traceable source and is left out. The three questions are my own distillation, not a quotation.]

Interruption without losing the thread

Clover is mid-way through the research and the wearer says "actually, never mind, what is the weather?" A new utterance is not a cancellation. It might be a quick aside, a correction to the running task, a pause, or a genuine stop, and each wants a different next move. I drafted a task-state model to tell them apart: answer the weather, keep the research running if that is appropriate, and never auto-resume a long spoken response while the wearer is busy.

Underneath it is the architectural decision I think matters most in the project. Task state lives separately from whatever is visible. A task can run while the display is completely clear. A task can fail while the wearer is in another interaction. Conversational intent, the task orchestrator and the presentation controller are three different things, and keeping them apart is what makes the experience fluid rather than an app with voice commands bolted on. This is proposed architecture, explored and diagrammed, not built end to end. [Confirm which behaviours were prototyped in flow-lab or the voice prototype and which stayed on paper.]

HUD // Rendering system

From designing individual interfaces to designing the rules that generate them.

Once the agent answers any request in any of three forms, a bespoke card for every capability stops scaling. So I looked at what Clover actually displays and found that almost all of it fits six ways of describing information: value, list, fields, series, comparison and matrix. These are not six apps or six card designs. A weather forecast and a stock price are both a series, so there is one reusable series component and the two capabilities share it.

The second half is a proposed rule, fit(primitive, data, priority), which returns the level of support that is appropriate, what should be shown, and what overflows, weighing the information's priority against the available space. "Show me my next twenty calendar events" is the test case: twenty do not fit, and instead of a calendar-specific solution there is one rule for what fits on the HUD. The exploration used a working area of about 448 by 328 pixels as its constraint, a design assumption rather than a verified hardware specification. Companion app // Design iterations

The app did not start in a garden. It started as spheres.

The founders wanted to defer the app to protect the hardware schedule, and their reasoning was sound: prototype cycles, supplier coordination and physical revisions cost real money. I argued that deferring it only moved the risk, because a customer does not experience a product along team boundaries. She wears the glasses on the commute, adjusts notifications on her phone, and reads the day's summary at night with the glasses off. I did not ask for equal engineering capacity. I asked that we define the cross-device experience early enough to find the gaps before the glasses were built.

Brand

Building the brand without a brand team, and testing every mark against the interface it had to live in.

The company had been using Spark without an articulated brand story, and without a brand there was no basis for consistent choices in the app. I produced 36 logo explorations and judged each by how it translated into interface elements. [confirm 36] The direction came from an ordinary moment: wearing the glasses in a garden, I noticed a four-leaf clover and read it as a good start to the day. Eden became the umbrella identity, with nature-based names for future hardware and Clover for the first product, built on my own reading of ikigai and the habit of noticing slower, ordinary moments, set deliberately against an industry that sells maximum speed.

INSERT IMAGE HERE: the 36-logo grid and the chosen mark

Two of my own choices failed in context and I changed them. The clean editorial typeface [Kyoto, confirm] that worked at phone scale was not legible as glanceable text on the glasses, so expressive brand type and functional display type were separated, with shared proportions and iconography. The first muted palette read as muddy across the whole iOS interface, so the app moved to a brighter palette with selective accents while the HUD stayed restrained. When internal feedback called the garden visuals too feminine for a male-skewing category, I brought the question back to whether the system communicated trust, approachability and difference to the audience we wanted. The nature-led direction was approved.

Design engineering

I learned to prototype the behaviour of an interaction, not just its appearance.

A conversational product has problems a Figma file cannot show: how people phrase a request, how the interface acknowledges speech, what happens in a pause or when the turn changes hands, how transcription should appear, how the visuals should move while someone is still talking, and how a wearer knows whether the agent is listening, processing or answering. So I taught myself Soniox, a real-time voice platform with speech-to-text and text-to-speech APIs, and wired it into the HUD explorations. That turned them into prototypes I could speak to, and made exploring the orb's conversational states far faster than drawing each one. A prototype should be as real as the uncertainty it is testing, and for conversation the timing and the feedback are the experience. Soniox handled voice processing only; it is not the model behind the agent's reasoning, and the voice prototype is a design tool, separate from production. Key takeaways and next steps

Design the rule, not the screen.

Design the rule, not the screen. Three levels and six primitives did more for the product than any single interface, because they decide every interface that follows.

Hold convictions lightly. I argued for app-first and revised it on evidence. I changed my palette and typography once I saw them in context. Revising a decision matters as much as the reasoning that led to it.

Challenge the scope without ignoring the business. The founders were right about manufacturing risk. I learned to argue for a second risk, an incoherent end-to-end experience, while respecting the first.

Match the prototype to the uncertainty. Static screens could not test a conversation, so I learned the tool that could.

Sync with engineering earlier than feels necessary. The most important conversations hide in the back of everyone's head. Align early and keep asking until everything is set out, rather than revisited.
