# Team 9, Research & Problem slides

## Slide 2: Territory

Title: Territory: service changes riders learn about too late

Foundational secondary research
- MTA app case study: service updates sit several taps deep and are buried under less important content, so riders learn of a change once they are already on the train (Robinson, MTA app design)
- City Comptroller data: bus service is as unreliable as the subway, and MTA dashboards report modest delay while riders experience longer waits, a gap between official data and lived reality (Time Out; MTA metrics)
- Amsterdam Bijlmer ArenA station: directional messaging at decision points reduced crossing flows and improved throughput after concerts; forecasting crowds beats reacting to them (CityFlows; SAIL forecasting study)
- Social plan apps: once a plan fails, people stop counting on it, so a single bad trip becomes a route never trusted again (Partiful, 222)

Why we think it is important
- Service changes concentrate on weekends and late nights, when riders have the fewest alternatives and the least information
- The capacity usually exists, a bus, a second station, a reroute, but the information arrives after the decision point
- A failed trip costs more than time: riders write off neighbourhoods and report it as preference, so the loss is invisible to the system
- An information fix is cheaper and faster than an infrastructure fix, and it is granular enough to solve well (Week 5 advice)

## Slide 3: Primary Research

Title: What we learned collectively about getting from A to B in NYC

Five interviews across segments
- Young professional, 23, former Columbia student: skip-stop change with no notice left him 45 blocks past his stop while ill; a Google Maps estimate failed 75 minutes into a trip, which he abandoned; he wrote off the Upper East Side and called it preference; never considered the bus
- Concertgoer, early 20s: crowds funnel to the nearest station entrance after events; she knows to use the 50th Street 1 stop, visitors do not; bike-cab solicitation adds stress to an already crowded exit
- Visitor, 22, from Texas: trains and buses that do not come; muffled audio announcements where visual displays would do; no cell service on long rides; navigates with a local friend
- Studio dweller, student: outside the transit territory, but her life is unbundled across the city, so a failed trip closes a room she depends on
- Event-crowd research: everyone heads for the closest entrance even when alternatives exist

Do we have an insight?
- Yes. The capacity was there in every story. The information was not, or it arrived once the rider was past the point of choice
- Locals compensate with familiarity. Everyone else abandons the trip, writes off the place, or follows a friend
- The system runs on knowledge riders already have rather than information it gives them at the moment of choice

## Slide 4: Problem Statement (tightened, same meaning)

Routine transit riders planning trips around weekend and late-night schedules struggle to anticipate route changes. Poorly communicated alerts reach them once they are already on the way, forcing them onto slow, unreliable detours and leaving them scrambling for alternatives that existed all along.

Alternative in the Week 5 form:
For: routine transit riders travelling on weekends and late at night.
Who need to: learn that a route has changed while they still have an alternative, because today alerts reach them mid-journey and the detour they are forced onto is slow and unreliable.
Design: a way to surface the change, and the alternative, before the decision point passes.
