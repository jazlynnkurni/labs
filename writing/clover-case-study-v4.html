Clover is not an interface that users navigate. It is an agent that negotiates how much interface the user needs at any given moment.

Founding product designer and design engineer, July to October 2026. I owned the whole experience of a pair of camera-free smart glasses and the iPhone app beside them: the interaction model, the rules that decide what the glasses show, the brand, the app, and the SwiftUI and voice prototypes I built to test it all.

1. Context

Smart glasses promise less time on your phone, and most of them deliver one more thing that interrupts you.

Clover is a personal AI agent in a pair of glasses with no camera, built to give immediate, contextual help without a lens pointed at anyone. We entered a category whose assumptions were already set, technical and intrusive and made for enthusiasts, and I joined before there was a settled interaction model, a design system or a brand. The founders were weighing voice, intent-led interaction, and an app-like model navigated on the glasses. The job was to decide what to display, where each interaction should live, and how the whole thing should feel.

2. Research

The category's visual language told you who it was for before you put the glasses on.

I compared Rokid, RayNeo, Even Realities and [others] on interaction mechanism, glanceability, information density and what the visual language signalled about the buyer. The utilitarian look they shared left room for something more approachable that gave up none of the utility. One detail mattered later: none of the successful brands imposes a fixed navigation scheme on the HUD.

The first usability round covered eight participants, split between technical and non-technical backgrounds. Three conversations are documented: a Columbia design professor, Anthony Fox, a data scientist, and Jonathan Henry Oliver, a trainee solicitor. [Add the other five session notes before any claim about all eight. Confirm the professor's name and quotations.]

  • The professor wanted notification summaries instead of long scrollable messages, the display lower in his field of view, and control over which apps appeared on it.
  • Anthony wanted a device nobody would notice, a structured timeline he could check off, and a clean line between what the glasses handle in the moment and what the phone manages.
  • Jonathan doubted the value for professionals who are already organised, worried the glasses would swap one screen for another, and was protective of his data. Those objections shaped the positioning more than the compliments did.

I treated the technical versus non-technical split as a hypothesis rather than proof that we needed two products. Across the documented sessions, control over interruption and a clear division between immediate and deeper tasks were better design signals than taste.

3. The app-first hypothesis, and what broke it

I chose app-first on purpose, loaded it on the glasses, and watched it fail at the first question of the day.

Voice or intent-led interaction can feel fluid, and it also hides what the product can do. An app-first structure is familiar and predictable. I proposed judging the models on learnability, predictability and cognitive load, then prototyped app-first as an explicit hypothesis. The first HUD followed the phone's mental model: the user has a task, navigates to an app or widget, issues a command, the AI answers inside that context, the app renders the response.

On the glasses that model inherited every assumption of a smartphone. It assumes you know what you want, know which application does it, and deliberately enter that environment. When you pick up your phone you usually do. When you put on glasses first thing in the morning you usually do not; you want direction, and the intention has not formed yet. The first question of the day is "what do I have today?", and the app-first HUD had no answer to a question that names no app. [The 86 percent dissatisfaction figure from live feedback stays qualitative until you can show the denominator and the measurement.]

So I pushed back on my own decision. The hybrid I moved to treats Notes, Calendar, Maps and Messages as services the agent invokes, not places the user goes: the user speaks naturally, the agent interprets intent and context, routes to the capability, and answers in whatever form fits, a spoken line, a glanceable card, a full view, or an action. To judge that half honestly I made the explorations voice-interactive with Soniox, a real-time speech-to-text and text-to-speech service, so I could speak to them instead of imagining the conversation; that work is in section 10.

4. Designing the rules of interaction

Once nobody navigates, the system has to decide when to show anything, what, and how much.

That question is the whole product. A phone leaves it to the user. The glasses cannot, so I wrote the rules.

Three levels of interface support

I stopped designing interfaces around application categories and started designing them around the amount of attention a task requires.

  • Level 1, ambient. A quick retrieval, a lightweight capture, an acknowledgement. One to five seconds, nothing opens. "When is my next meeting?" "Design sync at three."
  • Level 2, glanceable. A compact visual that helps the wearer verify, compare or decide. Five to twenty seconds. "What does my afternoon look like?" gets the next three events, condensed.
  • Level 3, expanded. A persistent surface for sustained workflows, editing, navigation or continuous guidance. As long as the task needs. "Help me rehearse my presentation" gets a teleprompter with live feedback.

The durations are design targets, not limits, and the levels describe the interface support a task needs, not the length of the spoken request. The point that took longest to land with the team is that these are not three fixed screens assigned to apps. One capability lives at all three. "How far is the nearest pharmacy?" is a spoken distance. "Navigate me to the pharmacy" is a persistent map. Both are Maps, and treating them as the same interaction is the smartphone assumption again. Level 3 has to be earned by the context and the intent, never assumed because the task is complicated.

Visual 02. The levels table and the capability-by-level grid. Source: `page-02.png`, `page-03.png`.

Multi-action orchestration

"Research stem cells, turn the findings into flashcards, and send them to Nita's mom." Three dependent actions in one breath. The instinct is to show three steps. I separated the orchestration from the presentation instead: a task layer coordinates the work in the background, and the HUD shows only the milestones that mean something to the wearer, the acknowledgement, a sense of progress, the result, and any confirmation that needs her. If she wants to inspect or edit the flashcards, the display earns Level 3. At any point she can say "hide this", "show me more" or "go back" and set the amount of interface herself.

The reasoning is about cognitive load and about the panel. Task complexity should not become interface complexity, and on a display this small it physically cannot. Progressive disclosure is the only way five internal operations fit a surface built for a glance.

Visual 03. The multi-action flow, natural-language request to orchestrated steps to review and confirm, and the three displays the user sees. Source: `page-04.png`.

Trust, partial success and consequential actions

"Put in my to-do for the product launch today at three, update my calendar, and text Rita to be ready." The first two actions are reversible, a to-do and a calendar event, so the agent can execute them straight away when the details are clear and the user has authorised it. The third reaches another person. That one deserves an explicit confirmation: "Your launch to-do and calendar event are set for 3 PM. I have drafted Rita's message. Ready to send?" One confirmation where the consequence is external, none where it is not. That keeps the agent from becoming reckless without turning every request into a confirmation loop.

Then the harder case. What if the calendar write fails while the to-do succeeds? The interface has to represent partial success plainly. Hiding a failure on a wearable is how trust ends, and so is "sorry, something went wrong", which leaves the wearer with no idea whether the earlier steps happened. Drawing on Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction, which ask systems to support efficient correction, communicate uncertainty and make their behaviour understandable, I reduced system state to three questions the HUD must be able to answer at any moment: what did you understand, what did you complete, and what still needs my attention. Recovery then happens locally, by clarifying or retrying the failed step, never by restarting the workflow. [The document's 88 percent wearable-abandonment statistic has no traceable source and is removed. The three questions are my distillation, not a quotation from Microsoft.]

An autonomous experience only works if the wearer keeps her understanding of, and control over, what the agent has done on her behalf.

Interruption without losing the thread

Clover is mid-way through the stem-cell research and the wearer says "actually, never mind, what is the weather?" A new utterance is not a cancellation. It might be a quick aside, a correction to the running task, a pause, or a genuine stop, and each wants a different next move. I drafted an explicit task-state model to tell them apart: answer the weather, keep the research running in the background if that is appropriate, and never auto-resume a long spoken response while the wearer is busy.

Underneath it is an architectural decision I think matters more than any screen in the project. Task state lives separately from whatever card, overlay or screen happens to be visible. A task can run while the display is completely clear. A task can fail while the wearer is in another interaction. Conversational intent, the task orchestrator and a presentation controller are three different things, and keeping them apart is what makes the experience fluid rather than an app with voice commands bolted on. This is proposed interaction architecture, explored and diagrammed, and it was not built end to end. The decision in four dimensions

To make the levels usable by an engineer rather than a designer's intuition, I reduced the choice to four questions: is the task one action or several; how much visual information does it need, none, a brief review or continuous guidance; is the wearer's attention limited, walking and multitasking, or available; and is the consequence low, information or personal capture, or external, sending or sharing. Those four answers pick the level. The same explorer also exposed the problem I would tackle next: three live sessions at once, navigation, live captions and a calendar reminder, is an attention problem, not a layout problem. Human attention follows one moving thing. You cannot park a map in one corner and captions in another the way a phone would.

5. Technical deep dive: rules that generate the interface

I moved from designing individual interfaces to designing the rules that decide how an interface is generated.

Once the agent answers any request in any of three forms, designing a bespoke card for every capability stops scaling. Every new skill would need its own components. So I looked at what Clover actually displays and found that almost all of its structured information fits six ways of describing data: a value, a list, a set of fields, a series, a comparison, and a matrix. These are not six apps or six card designs. A weather forecast and a stock price are both a series, so there is one reusable series component and the two capabilities share it.

The second half is a proposed function, fit(primitive, data, priority), which returns three things: the level of interface support that is appropriate, what should be shown, and what overflows. It weighs the information's priority against the available space and decides how to truncate or page. "Show me my next twenty calendar events" is the test case. Twenty do not fit, and instead of a calendar-specific solution there is one universal rule for what fits on the HUD. The exploration used a working area of about 448 by 328 pixels as its constraint; that is the number the layouts were measured against, not a verified final hardware specification.

Status: proposed and diagrammed, with the layout measurements worked through for the orb-plus-text and orb-plus-card cases. [Confirm whether any primitive or the fit rule was implemented in a lab.]

6. One product on two screens

The founders wanted to defer the app to protect the hardware schedule. I argued that deferring it only moved the risk.

Their reasoning was sound: prototype cycles, supplier coordination and physical revisions cost real money, and software looked like the thing that could wait. What I pushed back on was the assumption that it could wait without creating a different kind of risk. A customer does not experience a product along team boundaries. She wears the glasses on the commute, adjusts notifications on her phone, troubleshoots a connection, and reads the day's summary at night with the glasses off. I did not ask for equal engineering capacity. I asked that we define the cross-device experience early enough to find the gaps before the glasses were built.

  • On the glasses: fast, contextual, glanceable information and immediate hands-free requests.
  • On the iPhone: onboarding, configuration, app ordering, personalised Modes, richer research and search results, historical summaries and error recovery.
  • Across both: one agent identity, one set of preferences, continuity of memory and context, and a clear sense of where to go next.

The two interfaces should feel like one product and they should not copy each other. Consistency is shared behaviour and purpose, not identical screens.

7. The app: three versions, then the garden

The app did not start in a garden. It started as spheres.

My first direction was as utilitarian as the rest of the category, because the team assumed smart-glasses users valued function and technical sophistication above everything. Version one was an interface of interactive spheres, each standing for an action or capability of the agent. Version two set that aside for conventional, function-first layouts. Both communicated what the product could do, and in testing neither did much more than that; with the non-technical participants in particular they read as capable and cold. [Add dates, who saw V1 and V2, and what they said.]

That sent me back under the screens. Were we designing for the people who already buy smart glasses, or for the larger group we said we wanted? Rather than a third round of the same layouts I went back to positioning. Version three is the garden, drawn from the brand work in section 9: the four-leaf clover, ikigai, the habit of noticing small, ordinary moments. Its job is specific. The app has to carry the emotional side of the product, approachability and a sense of care, because the HUD, by its constraints, cannot. Utility stayed where it was. I would not call the garden better because it is more expressive; it is a hypothesis with a clearer audience than the first two, and it still needs validation with first-time, non-technical users, including whether the calmer treatment costs anything in scan speed.

AI on the phone as search, not a second chat

Copying the glasses' agent onto the phone would make the phone a second place for the same interactions. Removing AI would hide the agent's memory and reach. So the phone's AI is a search and exploration surface: longer questions, richer answers, follow-up, deliberate digging, in the quieter garden atmosphere. The glasses keep the immediate, hands-free moments. The phone opens up when someone chooses to spend attention.

Modes, and ordering the glasses by hand

Participants wanted control over what appeared on the glasses without digging through menus in the HUD. That became Modes in the app, focus configurations plus Do Not Disturb and Sleep, with drag-and-drop ordering of the apps shown on the glasses, so a person expresses priority directly. A minimal default for first-time wearers, deeper control on the phone for people who want it, and no separate HUDs for confident and less confident users. [Verify which Mode types and which drag-and-drop capabilities shipped.]

The Dynamic Island for small updates

Some updates do not deserve a screen. For a lightweight status, an evening summary that is ready, I designed concepts that use the Island as a compact, well-timed entry point. The constraints did the designing: very little width, truncation, timing, and staying useful at a glance. I treated it as a study of what Apple's platform allows, not a generic notification surface. 8. Brand, built without a brand team

Branding kept losing to hardware on the priority list, so I took it, and tested every mark against the interface it had to live in.

The company had been using Spark without an articulated brand story, and without a brand there was no basis for consistent choices in the app. I produced 36 logo explorations and judged each by how it translated into interface elements. [confirm 36] The direction came from an ordinary moment: wearing the glasses in a garden, I noticed a four-leaf clover and read it as a good start to the day. I explored Eden as an umbrella identity with nature-based names for future hardware and Clover for the first product, set deliberately against an industry that sells maximum speed.

Two of my own choices failed in context and I changed them. The clean editorial typeface [Kyoto, confirm] that worked at phone scale was not legible as glanceable text on the glasses, so expressive brand type and functional display type were separated with shared proportions and iconography. The first muted palette, right for an unobtrusive product in theory, read as muddy across the whole iOS interface, so the app moved to a brighter palette with selective accents while the HUD stayed restrained. When internal feedback called the garden visuals too feminine for a male-skewing category, I brought the question back to whether the system communicated trust, approachability and difference to the audience we wanted, and the nature-led direction was approved.

9. Design engineering

I learned to prototype the behaviour of an interaction, not just its appearance.

A conversational product has problems a Figma file cannot show: how people phrase a request, how the interface acknowledges speech, what happens in a pause or when the turn changes hands, how transcription should appear, how the visuals should move while someone is still talking, and how a wearer knows whether the agent is listening, processing or answering. So I taught myself Soniox, a real-time voice platform with speech-to-text and text-to-speech APIs, and wired it into the HUD explorations. That turned them into prototypes I could speak to, and it made exploring the orb's conversational states far faster than drawing each one. The reasoning was about fidelity: a prototype should be as real as the uncertainty it is testing, and for conversation the timing and the feedback are the experience. Soniox handled voice processing only. It is not the model behind the agent's reasoning, and the voice prototype is a design tool, separate from production. [Confirm what it included: synthesised replies, state animations, real or scripted responses. The orchestration, interruption and rendering rules in sections 4 and 5 were not implemented in it unless you say otherwise.]

On the phone I wrote the app in SwiftUI, a framework I did not know when the work started, learning the platform while making product decisions in it. I joined the engineers' technical discussions, learned the codebase, used AI-assisted tools to move faster, and worked on branches with pull requests. What it taught me is that a prototype is not fast if it leaves a large integration job behind it, so I now settle platform constraints and architecture with engineers before refining an interaction visually. 10. Outcomes, limits, and what I would measure

The project went from a loose set of HUD ideas and an unprioritised app to one direction across both screens, with the rules written down.

What exists: an intent-led HUD with three levels of interface support and a proposed orchestration, recovery and interruption model; a proposed rendering system of six primitives and a fit rule; a companion app with Modes shipped in SwiftUI; one nature-led visual system; and a voice-interactive prototype of the glasses' conversation. These are design and build outcomes, not evidence of market impact, and I would not present them as such.

The next round would measure what the document itself proposed: task completion rate, recovery success after a failed step, unnecessary clarification turns, time spent looking at the display, and whether wearers correctly understand which actions Clover completed. Alongside those, first-attempt task success on equivalent glasses and phone scenarios, and whether the app reduces or adds avoidable phone pickups. [Insert any verified results. What I learned

  • Design the rule, not the screen. Three levels and six primitives did more for the product than any single interface, because they decide every interface that follows.
  • Hold convictions lightly. I argued for app-first and revised it on evidence. I changed my palette and typography once I saw them in context.
  • Challenge the scope without ignoring the business. The founders were right about manufacturing risk. I learned to argue for a second risk, an incoherent end-to-end experience, while respecting the first.
  • Match the prototype to the uncertainty. Static screens could not test a conversation, so I learned the tool that could.
  • Shipping is a team outcome. A beautiful prototype is not the finish line; behaviour that runs and can be maintained on a real device is.
