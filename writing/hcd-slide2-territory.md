# Slide 2: Territory

Title: Territory: the trip that fails after you have already left

We started by asking what actually produces the feeling that New York is hard to get around. We assumed the answer was infrastructure: old tracks, slow buses, a park in the way. Our reading kept pointing somewhere else. In almost every account, the capacity existed. A bus ran. A second station was open. A reroute was posted somewhere. What failed was the information, and it failed at the one moment it was needed, after the rider had committed and before they could still change course.

Foundational secondary research
- A case study of the MTA app found service updates buried several taps deep, beneath content nobody was looking for, so riders learned of a change once they were already on the train. That is the shape of every story we gathered.
- City Comptroller data shows bus service is as unreliable as the subway, while the MTA's own dashboards report only modest delay. The official picture and the rider's experience do not match, and riders plan on the official one.
- Amsterdam's Bijlmer ArenA station cut crossing flows after concerts with directional messaging at the decision points themselves, and SAIL Amsterdam forecast crowds from live sensor and weather data rather than reacting after they formed. Both treat the moment of choice as the thing to design for.
- Writing on Partiful and 222 described hosts collecting twenty-four yeses and eight arrivals. Whether it is a train or a friend, once a promise fails you stop counting on it. A single bad trip becomes a permanent no.

Why we think this matters
- Service changes cluster on weekends and late at night, when riders have the fewest alternatives and the least information, and when the trip is usually the point of the evening rather than a commute.
- The cost is not the delay. One of our interviewees stopped visiting a neighbourhood that was closer to him than the places he went every week, and described it as preference. The system never saw the loss.
- Nobody is solving this at the moment it happens. The MTA posts the change, the map app estimates the time, and neither tells the rider, while they still have a choice, that the plan has broken.
- An information fix is granular enough to design well in one semester. We do not need to move a track. We need the right fact to arrive sixty seconds earlier.

Speaker notes
We chose this territory because our research kept contradicting our assumption. We expected an infrastructure story and found an information story. The Amsterdam case is the proof that it can be designed: messaging at the decision point changed behaviour without changing the station. The Partiful comparison is why we think it is human rather than technical: a failed promise, from a train or a friend, has the same effect on trust. Our interviewee who wrote off the Upper East Side is the person we are designing for.
