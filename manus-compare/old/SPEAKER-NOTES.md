## On the order

Research and the competitive gap come **before** the two failed attempts, even though attempt 1
actually happened first. That is deliberate: a case study that opens on a failure before the
audience knows the user reads as going backwards.

Slide 5 is explicitly marked **"attempt 1, before any of it"** and opens with *"all of that
came after we had already built the wrong thing."* Say that line. It keeps the timeline honest
while letting the research set up everything that follows.

## Slide 11 is the one that wins this

Most candidates present a success. You are presenting **two failures and what the second one
taught you**, and the insight is genuinely non-obvious: users ignored the role selector and
lingered on a pulsing map and a live countdown, which were **the only two things on the page that
moved**.

That is a designer reading behaviour rather than reading a survey. Sit on it. Let them react.

## Slide 14 is the one that reads most senior

A PM mentions a roadmap item in passing, and you recognise your tested, working layout is a
**maintenance cost disguised as a design**. You rebuilt it to be role-agnostic.

Name the cost out loud: you gave up precise control of a layout that had already tested well.
Naming the cost is what separates a trade-off from a win.

## Questions you will get

**"Where does the 30% to 65-70% come from, and over what window?"**
Live data from the in-group testing users at Manus, following the rollout of the AI-guided architecture, before the public launch. Say that on slide 19 before they ask. Be precise about measured-post-rollout versus observed in
testing, and say the window. If it is partly directional, say so first. **Volunteering the limit
is stronger than being caught at it.**

**"What exactly was yours versus the team's?"**
Sole designer. You led research, the design system, flows and interaction mechanisms. Engineers
built it, two PMs scoped it, one CMO and one strategist on positioning. Say that plainly.

**"Three months is short. What would you have done with more time?"**
Your own next steps answer it: lightweight feedback on AI decisions for transparency, and tracking
**time-to-first-meaningful-action** instead of time on site. That second one is a better metric
than the one you shipped against, and saying so is a strength.

**"How is this AI-first?"**
Both halves. In the product: an inference layer reading role, focus and mode from behaviour, with
a "why this matters" on every recommendation. In the process: Lovable for prototypes before
production code, and now parallel research agents and coded prototypes.

**"Isn't an AI recommending things just a feed?"**
No, and the difference is the explanation. "Prioritised by fit, not engagement" plus "why this
matters" is the whole design position: **an unexplained AI suggestion is a guess with confidence.**

## Module 2 — Autonomy and Influence, 30 min, interactive

They will interrupt. Let them. Have these four ready:

- **Decided without asking:** pivoting off gamification. The data said the framing was wrong, so
  you changed the problem rather than polishing the solution.
- **Changed your mind on evidence:** twice. Both pivots came from testing, not opinion.
- **Influenced a cross-functional partner:** the role-agnostic rebuild came from one PM comment,
  and you turned it into a system change. Then mapped the journey with PM and engineers so every
  surfaced opportunity had an owner and an API.
- **A constraint you accepted:** event submission stayed manual through Luma. You wanted it
  automated, engineering needed launch reliability, and you took the trade. **Knowing which fights
  to skip is the point of this round.**

**Note on their brief:** the Module 2 text says "as an engineering leader" and "partner to PMs".
That reads like boilerplate from a different role. Do not pretend to have led a team. Answer as
someone who drove the work and knew when to pull people in.

## Two cautions

- **Do not say you caused the acquisition or the ARR.** "Manus hit $125M ARR and was acquired by
  Meta while I was there" is true and impressive. Anything stronger invites a fight you lose.
- **Slide 14 mentions fabricated statistics your own research agents produced.** That is a
  strength, not a confession, but deliver it as method rather than apology: you audit, and the
  audit catches things.
