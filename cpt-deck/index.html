My time at Eden

Jazlynn Kurniandra · Founding Design Engineer
June to October 2026 · San Francisco and New York

What Eden is

Smart glasses with a tiny screen in the lens, and an assistant called Clover.

  • No camera. A small green display floats in front of one eye.
  • You speak to it. It answers in one short line you can read at a glance.
  • Clover is the assistant. It lives in the glasses and in an iPhone app.
  • The idea: help arrives before you ask for it.

What I was hired to do

Nothing existed yet. I built the brand, the app and the display rules from 0 to 1.

  • Decide how the display behaves when it speaks, what it shows, when it stays quiet
  • Design the iPhone app that goes with the glasses
  • Create the Eden and Clover identity 36 logo explorations, one chosen
  • Build prototypes the engineers and the manufacturer could use

What the work looked like

Seven stages from June to October, with two pivots, both from testing.

  • Studied the other smart glasses on the market Rokid, RayNeo, Even Realities, Google Glass, Meta Ray-Ban
  • Ran 8 usability sessions with technical and non-technical people
  • Put the glasses on 52 internal testers and watched what they did
  • Sketched, prototyped, tested, changed course, repeated
  • Synced weekly with industrial design, engineering, the AI researcher and both founders

What I found out

People wanted help without being interrupted.

“I avoid anything that labels me a technologist. I want hardware that is analog, quiet, and does not ask for my attention.”

Data scientist, technical tester, usability session

  • Testers gave quick moments to the glasses the next meeting, a fast answer
  • And everything deliberate to the phone calendar, settings, music, messages
  • My rule: proactive on the glasses, intentional in the app
  • Stay quiet until it is worth their attention
Eden · Clover5

The big change

We shipped a menu of apps. People just talked. So I rebuilt it around what they meant.

  • Version one: open an app, then ask inside it what my founder asked for first
  • 87% of the 52 testers spoke naturally from the start, not in app commands
  • Version two: say what you need, Clover works out which tool to use
  • “What do I have today?” went from 6 interactions to 2
  • The system grew from 30 use cases to 69

The two texts

One measures the cost of interruption. The other builds machines that interrupt on purpose.

Mark, Gonzalez and Harris, 2005

  • Watched 24 office workers in detail
  • 57% of their working spheres were interrupted
  • Most interrupted work was picked up again the same day, but only after more than two other activities
  • Attention was already fragmented before any software joined in

No Task Left Behind? Examining the Nature of Fragmented Work. CHI 2005.

Lu and colleagues, 2024

  • Train AI agents to offer help before anyone asks
  • A reward model that scores whether an offer would be accepted
  • A benchmark of 6,790 events to practise on
  • Unprompted help is treated as a capability to maximise

Proactive Agent: Shifting LLM Agents from Reactive Responses to Active Assistance. arXiv 2410.12361.

How my work sits between them

Clover is the agent the 2024 paper wants. My testers were the workers the 2005 paper describes.

From the 2005 paper

  • My testers already lived in that fragmented day. Every unprompted line on the display lands in it.
  • So the first design rule was about silence: stay quiet until it is worth their attention.
  • Quick moments on the glasses, deliberate work on the phone, decided by the wearer.

From the 2024 paper

  • Clover anticipates the task from what you say, which is the same move the paper trains for.
  • But my success metric ran the other way: fewer display interactions, not more offers of help.
  • The question I had to answer was not whether Clover could interrupt usefully. It was how often it should.

What I learned

Quiet is a feature.

  • Pick one north-star metric. Testing is noisy, one number keeps the design honest.
  • Pivots are the best part. Each one tests a hypothesis for real.
  • The best idea is rarely the first one.
  • Sync early and often. Design, engineering, research and founders all moved faster when they could see the same thing.
  • Measure the cost of attention, not just task success time looking at the display, clarifying questions nobody needed, phone pickups
